1. Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics summarize a data set by its centre, its spread and its shape.

  • Mean is the average and is pulled by outliers. Median is the middle value and resists outliers, so use it for skewed data such as income. Mode is the most frequent value.
  • Range = max − min. Sample variance s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) (divide by N for a population). Standard deviation s = √s², in the same units as the data.
  • Coefficient of variation CV = s / x̄ compares spread across data sets with different scales.
  • IQR = Q3 − Q1. Values below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR are flagged as outliers.
  • Skew: mean > median means right-skewed; mean < median means left-skewed.
  • Charts: histogram for one numeric variable, box plot for spread and outliers, scatter plot for two numeric variables, bar chart for categories.
Exam tipState whether you are using sample (n − 1) or population (N) formulas. Excel: AVERAGE, MEDIAN, STDEV.S, STDEV.P, QUARTILE.INC.

2. Probability

Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

  • Complement: P(A') = 1 − P(A)
  • Addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). For mutually exclusive events the last term is 0.
  • Multiplication rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A). For independent events this is P(A) × P(B).
  • Conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
  • Bayes' theorem: P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B). Use it to reverse a conditional, for example from "a defective part fails the test" to "a part that fails the test is defective".
Exam tipMutually exclusive and independent are different. Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so they are strongly dependent. For Bayes questions, draw a probability tree or a 2×2 table first.

3. Probability distributions

Binomial counts successes in n independent trials, each with success probability p. Check four conditions: fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independent trials.

  • P(X = k) = C(n, k) × pᵏ × (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
  • Mean = np; variance = np(1 − p)
  • Excel: BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE) for P(X = k); TRUE for P(X ≤ k)

Normal is the bell curve, described by mean μ and standard deviation σ.

  • Standardize with z = (x − μ) / σ, then read the standard normal table.
  • Empirical rule: about 68% of values fall within 1σ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ.
  • Excel: NORM.DIST(x, μ, σ, TRUE) gives P(X ≤ x); NORM.S.DIST for z-scores; NORM.INV to go from a probability back to x.
Worked exampleExam scores are N(70, 10). P(X > 85): z = (85 − 70) / 10 = 1.5, so P(Z > 1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668, about 6.7%.
Exam tipSketch the curve and shade the area you want before using the table.

4. Sampling and the central limit theorem

A sample statistic such as x̄ varies from sample to sample. Its distribution is the sampling distribution.

  • The mean of x̄ is μ. The standard error of the mean is SE = σ / √n (use s when σ is unknown).
  • Central limit theorem: for large n (a common rule is n ≥ 30), x̄ is approximately normal whatever the shape of the population. If the population is already normal, x̄ is normal for any n.
  • Sample proportion: mean of p̂ = p and SE = √(p(1 − p) / n). The normal approximation works when np ≥ 5 and n(1 − p) ≥ 5.
  • Larger n gives a smaller standard error. Quadrupling n halves it.
Worked exampleμ = 50, σ = 12, n = 36. SE = 12 / 6 = 2. P(x̄ > 53): z = (53 − 50) / 2 = 1.5, so the probability is about 0.067.
Exam tipStandard deviation describes individual values; standard error describes sample means. Using the wrong one is the most common mistake.

5. Confidence intervals

A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for a population parameter: estimate ± margin of error.

  • Mean, σ known (or large n): x̄ ± z × σ / √n
  • Mean, σ unknown: x̄ ± t × s / √n, with n − 1 degrees of freedom
  • Proportion: p̂ ± z × √(p̂(1 − p̂) / n)
  • Common z values: 90% → 1.645; 95% → 1.96; 99% → 2.576
  • Sample size for a mean: n = (z × σ / E)², where E is the margin of error you want. Round up.

Interpretation: a 95% interval means the method captures the true parameter in about 95% of repeated samples. It does not mean there is a 95% chance the true value lies in this one interval.

What changes the width: higher confidence widens it; larger n narrows it; larger σ widens it.

Worked examplex̄ = 200, s = 20, n = 25, 95% confidence. t (24 df) ≈ 2.064. Margin = 2.064 × 20 / 5 = 8.26, so the interval is about 191.7 to 208.3.

6. Hypothesis testing

A hypothesis test asks whether sample evidence is strong enough to reject a claim about a population.

  1. State H₀ (the claim, always with =, ≤ or ≥) and H₁ (the alternative: ≠, < or >).
  2. Choose the significance level α, usually 0.05.
  3. Compute the test statistic.
  4. Find the p-value, or the critical value.
  5. Decide: if p < α, reject H₀; otherwise fail to reject H₀. State the conclusion in business terms.
  • One mean, σ known: z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)
  • One mean, σ unknown: t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n), df = n − 1
  • One proportion: z = (p̂ − p₀) / √(p₀(1 − p₀) / n)
H₀ trueH₀ false
Reject H₀Type I error (probability α)Correct (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error (probability β)

p-value: the probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the sample, assuming H₀ is true. A small p-value is evidence against H₀. It is not the probability that H₀ is true.

Worked exampleA firm claims average delivery time is 30 minutes. A sample of 36 orders has x̄ = 32, s = 6. H₀: μ = 30; H₁: μ ≠ 30. t = (32 − 30) / (6 / 6) = 2.0 with 35 df. The two-sided p-value is about 0.053, so at α = 0.05 we fail to reject H₀ (a borderline result).
Exam tipNever write "accept H₀". Write "fail to reject H₀" or "insufficient evidence". Excel: T.TEST, T.DIST.2T, Z.TEST.

7. Regression and correlation

Correlation measures the strength of a linear relationship; regression fits a line to predict one variable from another.

  • Correlation r ranges from −1 to +1. Near ±1 is a strong linear relationship; near 0 is a weak or no linear one. The sign gives the direction.
  • Simple linear regression: ŷ = b₀ + b₁x, where b₁ = r × (s_y / s_x) and b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄.
  • Slope b₁ is the expected change in y for a one-unit increase in x. Intercept b₀ is the predicted y when x = 0 (meaningful only if x = 0 is realistic).
  • R² is the share of the variation in y explained by x. In simple regression R² = r².
  • Residual = y − ŷ. Good fits have residuals scattered randomly around zero.
  • Testing the slope: H₀: β₁ = 0. If the p-value is below α, x is a statistically significant predictor of y.
Worked exampleSales = 20 + 3.5 × advertising (in $1,000s). Spending $10,000 on advertising predicts sales = 20 + 3.5 × 10 = 55.
Exam tipCorrelation is not causation. Avoid predicting far outside the range of x in your data. Excel: CORREL, SLOPE, INTERCEPT, RSQ, or the Data Analysis ToolPak Regression output.
